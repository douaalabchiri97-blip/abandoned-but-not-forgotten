About this project

This atlas tells the stories of six pieces of NASA hardware left on the Moon and Mars, for school-age space explorers: what each was built to do, the science it made possible, what happened next, and how we know. It is a curated selection, not a complete inventory; the official brief also covers deep-space hardware, which this project leaves for future chapters. Teachers and researchers can open the data analysis near the end of the page.

The heart of the challenge · learn through stories

Six objects. Six stories of engineering and discovery.

Each chapter follows the same questions: What problem did this hardware solve? How did engineers use it? What did it help scientists learn? Why is it still worth remembering?

CHAPTER 01 · THE MOON · APOLLO 11

The reflector that still talks to Earth

A small array of mirrors became a long-running experiment between Earth and the Moon.

Mission jobAstronauts placed a laser retroreflector on the lunar surface.
Science made possibleObservatories send laser pulses and measure their return time to study the Earth–Moon distance and lunar motion.
Why it matters nowThe passive reflector still supports lunar laser-ranging measurements. It has no onboard power or moving parts; “still in use” describes the ongoing experiment, not an active spacecraft.

Evidence: NASA NSSDC equipment coordinates · NASA/ILRS lunar laser-ranging record

CHAPTER 02 · THE MOON · APOLLO ALSEP

A science station after the astronauts left

The astronauts only stayed a few days. Their science stations kept measuring after they left, until NASA switched the network off on 30 September 1977.

Mission jobApollo 11 deployed the earlier EASEP package; the expanded ALSEP stations were deployed on Apollo 12, 14, 15, 16 and 17.
Science made possibleDepending on the mission, instruments studied moonquakes, the lunar magnetic environment, solar wind, heat flow and other properties.
Why it mattersThe package shows how a human landing could leave behind a remote science station. “Left behind” does not mean useless: ALSEP produced valuable data for years, although its operations have ended.

Evidence: NASA Planetary Data System instrument-host record · NASA NSSDC site coordinates

CHAPTER 03 · THE MOON · APOLLO 15, 16, 17

The moon buggies that were parked for good

Three battery-powered rovers carried Apollo astronauts far beyond walking distance, then stayed behind.

Mission jobApollo 15, 16 and 17 crews drove Lunar Roving Vehicles to geology sites too far to reach on foot.
Science made possibleFaster travel let crews visit more places and bring back samples from more varied locations.
What happened nextEach rover was left on the Moon when its crew went home. NASA’s LROC orbiter-camera team has identified them in images.

Evidence: LROC coordinates of human features · NASA NSSDCA Apollo sites

CHAPTER 04 · MARS · PERSEVERANCE

The parachute that helped land a rover

A giant parachute slowed the spacecraft before the final powered descent to Jezero Crater.

Mission jobThe supersonic parachute reduced the spacecraft's speed during atmospheric descent.
Science made possibleIt helped deliver Perseverance safely so the rover could study an ancient lake-and-delta environment and search for signs of past microbial life.
What happened nextThe parachute was released during landing. HiRISE later imaged it on the Martian surface.

Evidence: NASA/JPL Mars 2020 landing press kit · HiRISE annotated surface image

CHAPTER 05 · MARS · SKY CRANE

The rocket-powered stage that flew away

The descent stage acted like a jetpack, lowering Perseverance on cables before flying away from the rover.

Mission jobEight throttleable engines slowed the vehicle; a sky-crane maneuver lowered the rover to the surface.
Engineering lessonLanding a heavy rover required several systems to work in sequence, autonomously, during a short communications blackout.
What happened nextAfter rover touchdown, the stage flew away and impacted at a safer distance. Orbital imagery shows its debris pattern.

Evidence: NASA/JPL landing sequence documentation · NASA/JPL HiRISE image

CHAPTER 06 · MARS · INGENUITY

The little helicopter that outlived its plan

A technology test became the first aircraft to fly on another planet.

Mission jobIngenuity travelled to Mars attached to Perseverance and was set down on the surface in April 2021 to test flight in Mars’s thin air.
Science made possibleIt was planned for up to five flights. It flew 72 times and scouted terrain ahead of the rover.
What happened nextAfter rotor damage on its 72nd flight in January 2024, NASA ended the mission. The helicopter still sits upright on the surface.

Evidence: NASA/JPL mission-end announcement (25 Jan 2024), as reported by NASASpaceflight · NASA/JPL debris-field image

Follow the mission · a short timeline

From the first lunar reflector to a Martian sky crane

1969

Apollo 11 places a laser retroreflector on the Moon.

1969–1972

Apollo 12, 14, 15, 16 and 17 crews deploy ALSEP science stations; from 1971, crews also drive Lunar Roving Vehicles.

2021

Perseverance lands in Jezero Crater; its landing hardware separates and stays on Mars. In April, Ingenuity is set down for its first flights.

2024

Ingenuity’s 72nd flight ends with rotor damage, and NASA ends its mission.

Today

Researchers continue to use some legacy instruments and study the traces of past missions.

The timeline is a learning aid, not a complete mission chronology. Dates and status are explained in the linked mission records.

How to tell a good hardware story

Five questions every object should answer

  1. What is it? Name the hardware and its mission.
  2. Why was it sent? Explain the engineering problem it had to solve.
  3. What did it make possible? Connect the hardware to a scientific observation or mission capability.
  4. What happened to it? Distinguish active, passive/in-use, shut down, jettisoned, and unknown states.
  5. How do we know? Link the claim to a NASA catalogue, mission document, dataset, or image—and label the evidence type.
Words to know

A mini space dictionary

  • Retroreflector: a mirror array that bounces light straight back where it came from.
  • ALSEP: a set of science instruments left on the Moon by Apollo crews.
  • Lunar Module: the lander that carried astronauts to the Moon. Its bottom half, the descent stage, stayed behind.
  • Sky crane: a rocket-powered stage that lowered Perseverance on cables.
  • Jettison: to release a part on purpose when it is no longer needed.
  • HiRISE / LROC: powerful cameras on NASA orbiters that photograph Mars and the Moon.
Why these stories fit the challenge: the focus is on the hardware itself and the science it made possible, explained for school-age space enthusiasts. The GIS explorer and evidence archive support the stories; they are not the story's replacement.
More hardware to explore

Other machines NASA left behind

🌕 On the Moon

  • Surveyor landers: robot probes of the 1960s that proved soft landings were possible.
  • Lunar Module descent stages: the lower halves of the landers, left as launch pads for the ascent stages.

🔴 On Mars

  • Sojourner: the first rover on Mars (1997), delivered by Pathfinder.
  • Spirit & Opportunity: twin rovers that worked for years and are now inactive.
  • Viking 1 & 2: the first U.S. landers to operate on Mars (1976).
  • Phoenix & InSight: Phoenix dug for water ice; InSight listened for marsquakes. Both lost power.

🚀 In deep space

  • Pioneer 10 & 11: early probes that explored the outer planets and drift out of the solar system.
  • Dawn: mapped Vesta and Ceres, then ran out of fuel and stays in orbit around Ceres.
  • NEAR Shoemaker: the first spacecraft to orbit and land on an asteroid, 433 Eros.
  • Kepler Space Telescope: hunted for planets around other stars, then ran out of fuel.

Summaries follow the NASA Data & Resources list for this challenge and the NSSDCA catalog, where each mission’s status can be checked.

Evidence archive

Explore documented hardware

Search by object, mission, or function. Each record identifies its evidence type, location precision, and primary source. Mars component records are image-based and do not claim individual coordinates.

Read the status labels: passive / still scientifically used = the object has no active power but supports measurements; operations ended = the instrument stopped collecting data; jettisoned / flew away = a landing component was deliberately released; site-level only = the source locates the landing area, not the individual object. These labels describe different histories—not one generic “junk” category.
For teachers & researchers · data analysis, method and limits
Comparative interpretation

Spatial evidence and equipment separation

The analysis is deliberately bounded: it measures separations only where NASA publishes paired Apollo coordinates, then compares the coordinate evidence with the image-based Mars records.

🌕 Apollo lunar surface

NASA NSSDC publishes separate coordinates for several categories of Apollo surface equipment, including Lunar Module sites, ALSEP packages, and lunar ranging retroreflectors (LRRR). This allows equipment-level plotting for the listed Apollo missions.

  • Multiple Apollo missions and surface experiments are represented.
  • Some records are scientific instruments; others are landing-stage hardware.
  • Coordinates use NASA's stated lunar reference system.

🔴 Perseverance at Jezero

NASA/JPL's HiRISE image identifies the parachute, backshell, descent stage, rover, and heat shield at the landing site. The four non-rover components are separated parts of one entry, descent, and landing sequence.

  • Evidence is image-based and spatially contextual.
  • Component locations are visible in the annotated image.
  • Individual component coordinates are not asserted in this prototype.

Reproducible spatial analysis · Apollo equipment pairs

For missions where NASA publishes both a Lunar Module (LM) site and an ALSEP/LRRR location, the application calculates great-circle surface separation using a spherical Moon radius of 1,737.4 km. These are approximate distances derived from published coordinates, not surveyed walking routes.

Distances are approximate great-circle surface separations, rounded to the nearest metre for readability. They are not walking routes, not positional-accuracy estimates, and not independent survey measurements. The Moon is treated as a sphere of radius 1,737.4 km.

Method & limitations

How this atlas was assembled

Reproducibility matters more than visual precision. Every plotted coordinate is tied to a stated source and reference frame.

1 · Source selection

Prioritized NASA/NSSDC, NASA/JPL and NASA mission documentation. Records were included only where a source identifies the equipment or its site.

2 · Location handling

Moon: use the published equipment coordinates and lunar reference system. Mars: show only the Jezero site reference point; do not infer component coordinates from a non-georeferenced screenshot.

3 · Classification

Classify hardware by mission role: landing structure, scientific package, ranging instrument, or entry/descent/landing component. “Left behind” is not automatically synonymous with waste.

4 · Comparison

Compare mission architecture and evidence type qualitatively. Avoid comparing raw counts across planets because the sample sizes and spatial precision differ.

5 · Verification

Each record links to a source. Coordinates are displayed with their source precision; site-level references are explicitly labelled as such.

6 · Limitations

This is a curated sample, not a complete inventory. It does not measure degradation, environmental impact, exact Mars component coordinates, or the total amount of material left by either programme.

Limits of the data

What the dataset does not claim

Clear boundaries prevent a visually polished prototype from overstating its scientific precision.

COORDINATE-BACKED

Fourteen lunar equipment records use the values published in NASA NSSDC's Apollo landing-site coordinate table, with the DE421 Mean Earth/Polar Axis lunar reference system explicitly named.

IMAGE-IDENTIFIED

Four detached Mars 2020 landing components are identified from NASA/JPL imagery. Their individual coordinates are left blank; the Jezero reference point is not substituted for their positions.

NOT A FULL GIS BASEMAP

The in-page Moon graphic is a coordinate plot, not a georeferenced raster or a full planetary GIS. NASA Moon Trek and Mars Trek are linked for authoritative imagery and layers.

CRS WARNING

Lunar coordinates are not EPSG:4326 / terrestrial WGS84. GIS software may assume Earth coordinates when opening generic GeoJSON; define an appropriate lunar CRS or use the CSV as a source table and retain the reference-system metadata.
